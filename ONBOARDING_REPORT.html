Drop Cube In Bin

Building a robot arm scene in MuJoCo, then trying to teach the arm to pick up a cube and drop it in a bin by showing it examples. The demonstrations work every time. The robot that learned from them never quite gets there, and working out why — including catching the simulator scoring a failure as a success — turned out to be the real content of the week.

Tasks 1 & 2 Panda arm · MuJoCo 3.3.4 October 2026

SummaryThe short version

Task 1 was building the scene: a table, a robot arm, a cube, and a bin. Task 2 was teaching the arm to do the pick-and-place job by copying a demonstration.

The part I did not expect: before any learning could happen, I had to fix four things in the starter files. Three made the task physically impossible — the gripper could not touch the cube, the cube was too big to pick up anyway, and the grip was too weak to hold it. The fourth was worse in its way: the simulator was loose enough to count a failed run as a success, so my own scores were wrong until I caught it.

After that, everything ran. The demonstrator solves the task 200 times out of 200. The neural network that learned from those demonstrations grips the cube about a third of the time, gets it a few centimetres off the table, and never finishes the job.

Task 1Building the scene

This part is mostly geometry. You write an XML file describing what exists and where it sits, and the physics engine builds it. I placed the table, mounted the arm on top of it, put a bin on the surface, and dropped a cube within reach.

The fiddly bit is that everything is positioned by its centre, and sizes are given as half the real thing. A box with size 0.05 is 10 cm wide, not 5 cm. I got that wrong and did not notice for a while, which turned into the first of the three faults below.

Task 2Four things that were broken

Task 2 is where the trouble started. I wrote the code to drive the arm, ran it, and the arm swept straight through the cube like it was not there. What followed was a few hours of measuring things rather than writing code.

1. The cube was too big to pick up

I had written the cube's size as 0.05, meaning to make a 5 cm cube. Because sizes are halves, I actually made a 10 cm cube. The gripper only opens 8 cm, and the bin is only 9 cm across inside, so the cube could not be grasped and would not have fitted in the bin either.

Fixed → 4 cm cube, which clears both limits comfortably.

2. The robot had collisions switched off

This one was in the supplied robot files, not mine. A single line near the top of the arm's description turned off collision for every part of the robot, including the fingertips. The arm was a ghost: it could move anywhere, through anything, and the gripper could never make contact with the cube.

I only found it by printing the collision setting for each part and seeing zeros all the way down. Comparing against the public reference version of the same robot showed that line belongs on the visual parts, not the whole robot.

Fixed → collision switched back on for the physical parts only.

3. The grip was too weak to hold anything

With collisions working, the gripper would close on the cube, lift, and the cube would slide straight back out. I printed the actual forces at each fingertip and found the problem: only one finger was ever pushing. One pad read 5 N while the other read 0.000 N, and the fingers settled around the cube with less than half a millimetre to spare rather than squeezing it.

The cause was a stiffness setting on the gripper. It capped the squeeze at about 2 N, which is not enough to hold a cube against gravity once the arm starts moving.

Fixed → stiffness raised 5×. Lift success went from 0 out of 7 to 7 out of 7.

4. The simulator was marking failures as successes

I found this one late, and only because the numbers stopped making sense. My results said the robot never lifted the cube and yet finished the job once in twenty tries. Both cannot be true, so I went back and replayed that one run.

The cube's highest point all run was 0.800 m. The bin wall tops out at 0.82 m. It never went over the wall — it went through it. The cube had been knocked across the table at 4.5 m/s, and the physics was running at an accuracy setting meant for fast batch training on a graphics card, too coarse to stop something moving that fast from passing through a 1 cm wall.

So my one success was not a success. Turning the accuracy up made that run fail, as it should have.

Fixed → accuracy raised; costs no extra run time here. Real score: 0 out of 20.
What this cost me

I spent a long time on the wrong suspects first: physics accuracy settings, friction on the fingertips, how fast the gripper closed. I tested all of them and none of them mattered. The thing that actually fixed it was the one number I had not thought to question, and I only got there by stopping and printing the contact forces instead of guessing again. That was the main lesson of the week.

Step 2The demonstrator

To teach a robot by copying, you first need something worth copying. The demonstrator is not learned at all — it is ordinary code that works out where the cube is, works out the joint angles needed to put the gripper there, and moves through the job in seven stages: go above the cube, come down, close, lift, move over the bin, open, wait for the cube to settle.

One snag worth recording. The arm's motors behave like springs pulling towards a target angle, so they always stop slightly short under the weight of the arm. Commanding the right position left the gripper 1–5 cm off, which is more than enough to miss a 4 cm cube. The fix was to measure the error and feed it back into the command until the gripper is within 2 mm.

Step 3Teaching the network to copy

The idea is simple. Every time the demonstrator does something, write down two things: what the robot could see at that moment, and what the demonstrator did about it. Do that 200 times and you have 111,200 of these pairs. Then train a small neural network to predict the second thing from the first. No rewards, no trial and error — it is just copying.

One gotcha caught me here. The arm's commands are angles, roughly between −3 and 3. The gripper's command runs from 0 to 255. Left alone, the network treats being slightly wrong about the gripper as hundreds of times more important than being wrong about the arm, so it learns the gripper beautifully and ignores the arm. Putting all the numbers on a comparable scale before training fixes it.

Training took 95 seconds on a laptop.

The network learned the copying task almost perfectly, and still never finishes the real job. Both of those are true at the same time, and the gap between them is the whole story.

AnalysisWhy it fails

When I first saw these scores I assumed I had a bug in the training. The numbers say otherwise. I retrained on 160 of the 200 demonstrations and tested on the 40 it had never seen: its answers there are 0.4% away from the demonstrator's, the same as on the ones it studied. It has genuinely learned the rule rather than memorised the answers. If the training were broken, that number would be bad too.

What goes wrong is what happens between answers. Each tiny error nudges the arm somewhere the demonstrator never went, so the next answer is based on a slightly unfamiliar situation, which produces a slightly bigger error. Over 556 steps that snowballs.

This task punishes that harder than most, because of something I measured back in fault 3: the fingers close around the cube with less than half a millimetre of room. The network's typical error is about half a millimetre — the size of the entire margin. That matches what the robot actually does. It grips the cube a third of the time, gets it up to 3.7 cm off the table, and needs 6 cm to clear the bin wall. It gets close and runs out of accuracy.

Then it gets worse in a specific way. Once the cube slips, the robot is looking at a situation that never appeared in its training data — no cube in the gripper — so its answers stop meaning anything, and it settles into holding one pose for the rest of the run. That is the frozen arm in the bottom row above.

I also checked a second possible explanation and ruled it out. I suspected the demonstrator might be giving contradictory answers in near-identical situations, which no copying method could untangle. Measuring it showed the answers in similar situations agree closely, so that is not the problem here.

NextWhat I would try next

  • Let it see its own mistakes. Add small random wobbles while recording the demonstrations, so the training data includes recovering from being slightly off, instead of only showing the perfect path.
  • Correct it as it goes. Let the robot attempt the job, have the demonstrator say what it should have done at each point it went wrong, and add that to the training data.
  • Make the grip more forgiving. Half a millimetre of margin is an unusually harsh target. A slightly smaller cube would make the whole task far more tolerant.
  • More demonstrations. Cheap to try at a third of a second each, though I would expect this to help least — the problem is not a shortage of examples.

NotesSetup and timings

Everything above ran on a laptop. Collecting 200 demonstrations took 59 seconds, training took 95 seconds, and installing the machine learning library took 50 seconds — about three and a half minutes of actual computing for the whole experiment.

Three setup problems worth passing on. The motion planning library the task suggests has no Mac version at all, so I wrote a stand-in that does the same job, and the notebook now uses whichever is available. And a stray unused import in the starter notebook crashes the whole thing the moment the machine learning library loads, because the two bring conflicting copies of the same underlying component. Deleting the unused line fixes it.

And the physics accuracy setting that caused fault 4 was in the supplied scene file; raising it cost no measurable run time here, so there is no reason to leave it low.

The fixes matter beyond my own run: anyone picking this up needs the corrected robot files, not just the scene file, or they will land right back on a gripper that passes through the cube — and on a scoreboard that cannot be trusted.
